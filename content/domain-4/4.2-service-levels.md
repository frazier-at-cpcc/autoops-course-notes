### Uptime and availability
- ==Uptime==: the share of a time window when the service works for its users
  - Written as a percentage, often in "nines": 99.9% is three nines
- ==Availability== = uptime / (uptime + downtime)
- Each extra nine cuts the allowed downtime by a factor of ten
  - And usually multiplies the cost: redundancy, automation, on-call staff
- The table below assumes a 30-day month and a 365-day year

| Uptime | Per day | Per month | Per year |
|---|---|---|---|
| 99% | 14m 24s | 7h 12m | 3d 15h 36m |
| 99.5% | 7m 12s | 3h 36m | 1d 19h 48m |
| 99.9% | 1m 26s | 43m 12s | 8h 45m 36s |
| 99.95% | 43s | 21m 36s | 4h 22m 48s |
| 99.99% | 9s | 4m 19s | 52m 34s |
| 99.999% | 1s | 26s | 5m 15s |

- Components in a chain multiply
  - An app at 99.9% that needs a database at 99.9%: 99.9% x 99.9% = 99.8%
  - Two redundant copies, either of which can serve: 1 - (0.001 x 0.001) = 99.9999%

### SLIs, SLOs, and SLAs
- ==Service-level indicator (SLI)==: the measurement
  - Good events divided by valid events: successful requests / all requests, requests under 300 ms / all requests
- ==Service-level objective (SLO)==: the internal target for an SLI over a window
  - "99.9% of checkout requests succeed, measured over a rolling 30 days"
  - Set by engineering and product; missing it changes priorities, not invoices
- ==Service-level agreement (SLA)==: a contract with customers that names an availability level and a penalty
  - Usually ==service credits==: a percentage of the monthly bill refunded
  - Defines what counts: scheduled maintenance and customer-caused outages are usually excluded
- Keep the SLO stricter than the SLA
  - The gap is a warning zone: you miss your own target before you owe anyone money

:::wide
| Term | What it is | Example | Who holds you to it |
|---|---|---|---|
| SLI | a measured ratio | 999,412 of 1,000,000 requests returned 2xx | dashboards and alerts |
| SLO | target for the SLI | 99.9% success over 30 days | your own team |
| SLA | contract with consequences | 99.5% monthly or a 10% credit | customers and lawyers |
:::

### Error budgets
- ==Error budget== = 1 - SLO
  - A 99.9% SLO over 5 million requests allows 5,000 failed requests
  - Over a 30-day month as time: 43 minutes 12 seconds
- Budget left: ship features, take deployment risks
- Budget spent: freeze risky releases and spend the time on reliability
  - Agree on this policy before the outage, not during it

```python label="budget.py"
slo = 0.999                 # 99.9% of requests
good, total = 4_996_210, 5_000_000

sli = good / total
allowed = (1 - slo) * total # failures allowed
used = (total - good) / allowed
print(f"SLI          {sli:.4%}")
print(f"SLO met      {sli >= slo}")
print(f"budget       {allowed:,.0f} bad requests")
print(f"budget used  {used:.1%}")
```

```console label="Terminal"
$ python3 budget.py
SLI          99.9242%
SLO met      True
budget       5,000 bad requests
budget used  75.8%
```

### MTTR and MTBF

- ==Mean time to repair (MTTR)==: average time from failure until service is restored
  - MTTR = total downtime / number of failures
  - Includes detecting, diagnosing, fixing, and verifying; mean time to detect (MTTD) is often tracked on its own
  - The R is also read as recover, restore, or resolve; check which one a report means
  - Lower it with good alerts, runbooks, automated rollback, and practiced on-call staff
- ==Mean time between failures (MTBF)==: average working time between one failure and the next
  - MTBF = total uptime / number of failures
  - Higher is better; raise it with testing, redundancy, and removing known causes
- Mean time to failure (MTTF) is for parts that are replaced, not repaired: disks, fans, optics
- Tie them together: availability = MTBF / (MTBF + MTTR)
  - Cutting MTTR in half helps as much as doubling MTBF

:::figure wide 4.2-mtbf-mttr.svg | MTBF averages the purple stretches between failures; MTTR averages the rust stretches spent restoring service.

```python label="mtbf.py"
window_h = 30 * 24             # 720 hours
outages_min = [42, 18, 30]     # three incidents
n = len(outages_min)

mttr_h = sum(outages_min) / n / 60
uptime_h = window_h - sum(outages_min) / 60
mtbf_h = uptime_h / n
avail = mtbf_h / (mtbf_h + mttr_h)
print(f"MTTR          {mttr_h * 60:.0f} min")
print(f"MTBF          {mtbf_h:.1f} h")
print(f"availability  {avail:.3%}")
print(f"check         {uptime_h / window_h:.3%}")
```

```console label="Terminal"
$ python3 mtbf.py
MTTR          30 min
MTBF          239.5 h
availability  99.792%
check         99.792%
```

- Worked by hand: 90 minutes down over 3 failures is a 30-minute MTTR
  - 720 h minus 1.5 h down = 718.5 h up; 718.5 / 3 = 239.5 h MTBF
  - 239.5 / (239.5 + 0.5) = 99.792%, the same as 718.5 / 720
- 99.792% misses a 99.9% SLO: the error budget was 43.2 minutes and the month used 90

### Feedback loops
- A ==feedback loop== carries what production teaches back into development

:::figure 4.2-feedback-loop.svg | Production data flows back into the next round of work. Yellow steps are where the loop learns something.

- Monitoring and alerts measure the SLIs and burn rate
- Incidents end in a blameless postmortem
  - Action items go into the backlog with an owner and a due date
  - The bug that caused it becomes a regression test
- Error budget status sets the release pace for the next window
- Delivery metrics close the loop on the pipeline itself
  - The DORA metrics: deployment frequency, lead time for changes, change failure rate, and time to recover from a failed deployment
- User signals count too: support tickets, feature usage, app store reviews

:::exam
- SLA is the external contract with penalties; SLO is the internal target; SLI is the measurement. A distractor will swap SLA and SLO.
- MTTR measures how fast you recover, MTBF how often you fail. "Reduce the time to restore service" means lowering MTTR.
- Expect to convert a percentage into allowed downtime: 99.9% is about 43 minutes per month and 8.76 hours per year.
:::
